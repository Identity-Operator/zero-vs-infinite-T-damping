\begin{abstract}
Randomized compiling makes hardware noise easier to handle by twirling it into
Pauli noise, but it also removes the pull of amplitude damping toward
$\ket{0}$. This has been reported to make variational models worse, and
reversing the direction of the damping to make them worse still. We ask what
these effects really are. Comparing amplitude damping with its Pauli twirl,
which is the same damping at infinite temperature, we find that the twirl
mainly flattens the measured expectation values across inputs. A trained output
scale undoes most of the loss in accuracy, so the models learn just as well,
but the price returns as measurement shots. Under the twirl the flattened
values must be measured far more often, while under amplitude damping a floor
keeps them resolvable. This floor vanishes as the temperature rises, and at
realistic damping strengths it matters only in very deep circuits. We also show
that reversing the damping is usually a gauge freedom that changes only the
parameters, and that the reported direction effect arises only because the
damping sits inside a decomposed two-qubit gate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Variational quantum algorithms and quantum machine learning models are built
from parameterized circuits and are meant to run on noisy near-term
devices~\cite{preskill2018nisq,cerezo2021variational,bharti2022noisy}. They can
only be trained if their outputs change with the parameters. In deep random
circuits, this change vanishes exponentially with the number of qubits, a
problem known as a barren
plateau~\cite{mcclean2018barren,cerezo2021cost,larocca2025barren}, and noise
makes it worse. Unital noise drives the state toward the maximally mixed state,
so local expectation values decay exponentially with
depth~\cite{wang2021noiseinduced,stilckfranca2021limitations}. Non-unital noise
behaves differently. Amplitude damping (AD) drives the state toward a pure state
and avoids barren plateaus for local observables~\cite{mele2026noise}, although
the output then forgets the early
layers~\cite{schumann2024emergence,singkanipa2025beyond}.

This difference matters in practice, because hardware noise is often made
unital on purpose. Randomized
compiling~\cite{wallman2016noise,hashim2021randomized} averages the noise over
random Pauli gates, which turns AD into its Pauli twirl. The twirl keeps the
shrinking of AD but removes its pull toward $\ket{0}$, leaving generalized
amplitude damping at infinite temperature. Comparing AD with its Pauli twirl
therefore isolates the pull toward $\ket{0}$, the zero-temperature bias of the
damping. A recent study~\cite{vanrossum2026biased} made this comparison and
found that twirling degrades eigensolver solutions and reduces the range of
outputs a model can fit, and that reversed damping performs worse than ordinary
damping. What these effects mean is less clear. They could reflect a real loss
in what the model can learn, or only a change in how its outputs must be read
out. It is also unknown whether the direction of the damping matters in itself.

In this work, we show that in our models the zero-temperature bias acts mainly
through the spread of the measured expectation values over the inputs. Under
AD, this spread settles on a floor, whereas under the twirls it keeps shrinking
with every layer. The floor vanishes as the temperature rises. A trainable
readout absorbs most of the resulting differences in accuracy, much as
rescaling methods of error mitigation do~\cite{vovrosh2021simple}, so the
twirled models lose little in what they can learn. The difference reappears
instead as a cost in measurement shots~\cite{wang2024errormitigation}: at a
fixed shot budget, the classifier under AD stays close to its noiseless
accuracy, while the twirled classifiers fall far below it. At weak damping, the
two separate only in very deep circuits.

We also show that the direction of the damping is often a gauge freedom.
Reversing the damping changes the gates, the input state, and the measured
observables as well. When the damping follows complete entangling layers and
the circuit has trainable boundaries, these changes are absorbed by a Pauli
frame or by spin time reversal, and reversing the damping
amounts to a change of parameters. This extends the parameter symmetries of Fontana
\textit{et al.}~\cite{fontana2022nontrivial} from one circuit to a pair of
noise models. Time reversal leaves every single-qubit rotation unchanged, so
only multi-qubit gates, the placement of the noise, and the circuit boundaries
can make the direction physical.

We organize the text as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setting} introduces the
channels and models. Section~\ref{sec:scale} treats the output scale,
Sec.~\ref{sec:gauge} the gauge, and Sec.~\ref{sec:cost} the spread, the
sampling cost, and their dependence on the damping strength.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Channels and models}
\label{sec:setting}

In this section, we introduce the noise channels that we compare and the three
models on which we compare them: single-qubit re-uploading fits, a four-qubit
classifier, and a three-qubit eigensolver.

\subsection{Channels}
\label{sec:channels}

We describe each single-qubit channel $\mathcal N$ through its action on the
Bloch vector, which is an affine map $\mathbf r\mapsto T\mathbf r+\mathbf t$~\cite{nielsen2010quantum}, with
\begin{equation}
T_{ij}=\tfrac12\Tr\bigl[P_i\,\mathcal N(P_j)\bigr],\qquad
t_i=\tfrac12\Tr\bigl[P_i\,\mathcal N(I)\bigr],
\label{eq:bloch}
\end{equation}
where $P_i\in\{X,Y,Z\}$. The matrix $T$ describes how the channel shrinks the
Bloch vector, and the vector $\mathbf t$ how it pushes it. The channel is
unital if and only if $\mathbf t=\mathbf 0$.

We compare amplitude damping with two twirled versions of it. Amplitude damping
of strength $p$ does two things to the Bloch vector. It shrinks the vector, by
$c=\sqrt{1-p}$ in the transverse directions and by $c^2$ along $z$, and it
pushes the vector toward $\ket0$ by $p$. The Clifford twirl~\cite{dankert2009exact}
averages these effects over all directions and leaves depolarizing noise, which
shrinks every component by the same factor and pushes nowhere. The Pauli
twirl~\cite{dur2005standard,wallman2016noise} is gentler. It keeps the
shrinking of AD exactly but removes the push toward $\ket0$, the non-unital
term.

The Pauli twirl can also be seen as an average over the two directions of the
damping. Reversed damping, $\AD^{\rm flip}$, is AD conjugated by a bit flip and damps
toward $\ket1$, and the twirl is
\begin{equation}
\AD_{\rm Pauli}=\tfrac12\bigl(\AD+\AD^{\rm flip}\bigr).
\label{eq:twirl_mix}
\end{equation}
This average has a simple physical meaning. Generalized amplitude
damping~\cite{nielsen2010quantum} with excited-state population $N$ has the
same $T$ as AD and $t_z=p(1-2N)$. AD, its Pauli twirl, and AD$^{\rm flip}$ are
the cases $N=0$, $\frac12$, and 1, so comparing AD with its Pauli twirl compares
damping at zero and at infinite temperature.

We treat the twirled channels as idealized, replacing the channel by its twirl
at every insertion. No twirling protocol can do this exactly after a
non-Clifford gate such as the data encoding, so the twirled channels should be
read as model channels that share the contraction of AD.

\subsection{Single-qubit re-uploading fits}
\label{sec:model_vr}

Our first model is the single-qubit data re-uploading model of
Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased}. With $\mathcal U(V)\colon\rho\mapsto V\rho
V^\dagger$, the encoding $\mathcal S_x=\mathcal U(R_X(x))$, and trainable
blocks $\mathcal W_\ell=\mathcal U(R_ZR_YR_Z)$ with three parameters each, the
noisy circuit with $L=2$ layers is
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{x,\theta}=\mathcal N\circ\mathcal W_2\circ\mathcal N\circ\mathcal S_x
\circ\mathcal N\circ\mathcal W_1\circ\mathcal N\circ\mathcal S_x\circ\mathcal N
\circ\mathcal W_0 ,
\label{eq:vr_map}
\end{equation}
and the raw model outputs
\begin{equation}
f(x)=\Tr\bigl[Z\,\Phi_{x,\theta}(\ket0\bra0)\bigr].
\label{eq:vr_raw}
\end{equation}
To measure expressivity, we fit random targets and record the largest range
$R$ for which Adam reaches a mean squared error below $10^{-5}$ within 150
steps. As targets, we use 200 random degree-2 trigonometric polynomials, each
rescaled to range $R$ and the same for every channel. We then compare the raw
model with a rescaled model $f_{s,b}(x)=s\,f(x)+b$, in which $s$ and $b$ are
trained together with the circuit. Appendix~\ref{app:training} gives the remaining
details of the protocol.

Our second model is a data re-uploading
classifier~\cite{perezsalinas2020data,schuld2021encoding} on $n=4$ qubits
(Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit}), which classifies MNIST
images~\cite{lecun1998gradient} of the digits $\{1,3,5\}$. Each image is
reduced to four principal components, which are mapped linearly to angles
$a_j\in[0,2\pi]$. As a second data set, we
use three classes of Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion}.

The circuit is built from three ingredients, namely trainable blocks, the
encoding, and a ring of CNOTs,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal R^{(\ell)}={\textstyle\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}}R_Z R_Y R_Z,\quad
S(\mathbf a)={\textstyle\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}}R_X(a_j),\\
E=\mathrm{CNOT}_{n,1}\cdots\mathrm{CNOT}_{2,3}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{1,2},
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:blocks}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CNOT}_{j,k}$ has control $j$ and target $k$. Each layer applies a
trainable block and the ring, then the encoding, with the channel acting after
both,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L^{(\ell)}_{\mathbf a}=\mathcal N^{\otimes n}\circ
\mathcal U\bigl(S(\mathbf a)\bigr)\circ\mathcal N^{\otimes n}\circ
\mathcal U\bigl(E\,\mathcal R^{(\ell)}\bigr),
\label{eq:layer}
\end{equation}
and the full circuit is
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\mathbf a,\theta}=\mathcal N^{\otimes n}\circ\mathcal U\bigl(\mathcal R^{(L)}\bigr)
\circ\mathcal L^{(L-1)}_{\mathbf a}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal L^{(0)}_{\mathbf a},
\label{eq:circuit}
\end{equation}
with $2L+1$ insertions of the channel per qubit and $\theta$ the $3n(L+1)$
trainable angles of the blocks. The readout acts on the
single-qubit expectation values
\begin{equation}
z_j(\mathbf a,\theta)=\Tr\bigl[Z_j\,\Phi_{\mathbf a,\theta}
\bigl(\ket0\bra0^{\otimes n}\bigr)\bigr],
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
and the scores of the three classes are
\begin{equation}
\ell(\mathbf a)=
\begin{cases}
W\mathbf z+\mathbf b & \text{(raw)},\\
W\,\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\sigma)^{-1}(\mathbf z-\boldsymbol\mu)+\mathbf b
 & \text{(standardized)},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
where $W$ is a trainable $3\times n$ weight matrix and $\mathbf b$ a trainable
bias. In the standardized readout, $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\sigma$
collect the means $\mu_j$ and the population standard deviations $\sigma_j$ of
the $z_j$ over the 1000 training images. We recompute them at every
training step, with gradients flowing through them, and apply their values at
the final parameters to the test images. The standardized readout adds no
parameters to the raw one. It only fixes the scale at which $W$ acts.

Unless stated otherwise, we use $p=0.3$ and simulate the density matrix
exactly, so the expectation values carry no sampling error. The exception is
the finite-shot runs, in which each $z_j$ carries the noise of $N_s$
measurements, in training as well as at test time. For these runs, we define
the operational shot cost of a trained model as the smallest $N_s$ at which its
mean test accuracy lies within 0.01 of its accuracy with exact expectation
values, and we report its geometric mean over the initializations. Training
settings, classical baselines, and the shot model are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

\subsection{Three-qubit variational eigensolver}
\label{sec:model_vqe}

Our third model is the eigensolver of Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased} for the
periodic transverse-field Ising chain on three qubits,
\begin{equation}
H=-J\sum_{i=1}^{3}Z_iZ_{i+1}-h\sum_{i=1}^{3}X_i ,
\label{eq:tfim}
\end{equation}
with $J=1$, $h=0.5$, and $Z_4\equiv Z_1$, whose ground-state energy is
$E_0=-3.2321$. The ansatz starts with a trainable $R_Y$ on each qubit, followed
by four blocks. Each block applies a trainable $R_{ZZ}(\phi)$ to each of the three pairs and a
trainable $R_X$ to each qubit, and each two-qubit gate is decomposed as
\begin{equation}
R_{ZZ}(\phi)=\mathrm{CNOT}_{i,i+1}\,\bigl[I\otimes R_Z(\phi)\bigr]\,
\mathrm{CNOT}_{i,i+1} .
\label{eq:rzz}
\end{equation}
We treat single-qubit gates as noiseless and write the noise strength of the
eigensolver as $\gamma$.
Since the placement of the noise turns out to matter, we compare two placements
with the same number of insertions. In their \textit{inside}
placement, the channel acts on both qubits
after each CNOT. In the \textit{after} placement, it acts twice on both qubits
after the complete $R_{ZZ}$. For each noise strength and placement, we minimize
the energy from 50 initializations, the same for every channel, and report the
relative error of the energy of the output state $\rho(\theta)$ with respect
to $E_0$. Optimizer settings and a cross-check against their code are given in Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

\section{Output scale}
\label{sec:scale}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_vr}
\caption{Single-qubit re-uploading fits of Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased}
(200 targets). (a) Largest converging output range of the raw model
$f=\langle Z\rangle$, with a free target offset. (b) Fraction of targets of range $R=1$ that
converge within 1000 steps when an output scale $s$ and offset $b$ are
trained. (c) Shot factor $\kappa=(s/s_{\rm None})^2$ at $R=1$ from the median
fitted scale; the dotted line is the exact depolarizing value $\lambda_{\rm depol}^{-10}$. Bars
are 95\% confidence intervals of the mean over targets in (a) and Wilson 95\%

\label{fig:vr}
\end{figure*}

We begin with the simplest way in which noise can change a variational model:
by changing the scale of its output. AD and its Pauli twirl, damping at zero
and at infinite temperature, share the same contraction matrix $T$. Any
difference in scale between them must therefore come from the non-unital vector
$\mathbf t$, which carries the zero-temperature bias (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}). In this section, we ask
how much of the effect of the noise a trainable output scale can undo, and what
it costs to undo it.

\subsection{Scale and sampling cost}

Consider first the simplest case, in which the noise only rescales and shifts
each expectation value,
\begin{equation}
\tilde z_j=s\,z_j+b_j ,
\label{eq:affine}
\end{equation}
with constants $s\neq0$ and $b_j$ that depend on neither the input nor the
parameters. A trainable affine readout can then undo the noise completely:
every classifier that it can build from the noiseless $\mathbf z$, it
can also build from the noisy $\tilde{\mathbf z}$, so the noise leaves
the model class unchanged. A single qubit under depolarizing noise is an exact
example. Because this channel commutes with every unitary, $N_{\rm ins}$
insertions simply multiply the output by a constant,
\begin{equation}
\tilde f=\lambda_{\rm depol}^{N_{\rm ins}}f ,
\label{eq:depol_scale}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\rm depol}=\frac13(2c+c^2)$ is the factor by which depolarizing
noise shrinks each Bloch component, the average of those of AD.
For anisotropic or non-unital channels, or on more than one qubit, the noisy
model is no longer a rescaled copy of the noiseless one. Whether the scale
still accounts for most of the difference then becomes an empirical question,
which we test in the rest of this section. The average behavior of the spread
on several qubits, its per-layer decay under the twirls and its floor under
AD, is derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} and Appendix~\ref{app:width}.

On hardware, however, rescaling is not free. The number of shots is one of the
main costs of variational algorithms~\cite{wecker2015progress,gonthier2022measurements},
and an expectation value estimated from $N_s$ shots, $\hat z_j$, has the variance
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\hat z_j)=\frac{1-z_j^2}{N_s}\le\frac1{N_s} .
\label{eq:shotvar}
\end{equation}
The standardized readout divides each $z_j$ by its spread $\sigma_j$, so the
shot noise of the standardized $z_j$ has variance
$(1-z_j^2)/(N_s\sigma_j^2)\le1/(N_s\sigma_j^2)$. The smaller the spread, the more
the shot noise is amplified. Keeping the class scores equally precise therefore
requires $N_s\propto1/\sigma^2$, and a channel $\mathcal N$ needs about
$\kappa(\mathcal N)$ times the shots of the noiseless circuit, with the shot
factor
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\mathcal N)=\biggl(\frac{\sigma(\text{None})}{\sigma(\mathcal N)}\biggr)^{2},
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the spread $\sigma_j$ at the trained parameters, averaged over
the qubits and the initializations.
For the single-qubit fits, we use $1/\lvert s\rvert$ in place of $\sigma$, with
$s$ the median fitted output scale, so that $\kappa=(s/s_{\rm None})^2$. In the language of error
mitigation, the trained readout performs a
rescaling~\cite{vovrosh2021simple,urbanek2021mitigating}. Such a rescaling
cannot undo an exponential concentration without an exponential number of
shots~\cite{wang2024errormitigation}. In Sec.~\ref{sec:shots}, we test how well
$\kappa$ predicts the operational shot cost of Sec.~\ref{sec:model_clf}, the
number of shots that a trained classifier actually needs.

\subsection{Single-qubit fits}
\label{sec:vr}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_clf}
\caption{Four-qubit classifier on MNIST against the number of re-uploading
layers. (a) Test accuracy with the raw readout and (b) with the

Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa} with the standardized readout. Panels (a) and (b) show the

\label{fig:clf}

We now examine the single-qubit fits. With
the raw output and a free target offset, we recover the reported ordering at
$p\ge0.1$ [Fig.~\ref{fig:vr}(a)]. At $p=0.1$, for example, the largest
converging range is 0.86 for AD, compared with 0.77 and 0.75 for the Pauli and
Clifford twirls. This range is not a clean measure of expressivity,
however. The noiseless model reaches only 0.65, below every noisy channel at
$p\le0.1$. The metric therefore measures how quickly the model converges within
the 150-step budget as much as the range it can reach. Indeed, when we center
the targets on zero, the ordering at $p=0.1$ reverses (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

Once the model can train its own scale and offset, the difference between the
channels disappears [Fig.~\ref{fig:vr}(b)]. We train at two fixed ranges, $R=1$
and $R=0.468$, the latter being the median largest range of the noiseless raw
model. At $R=1$, AD converges within 1000 steps on 96.5 to 99.0\% of the
targets for $p=0.05$ to 0.2, compared with 98.5 to 99.0\% for the Pauli twirl
and 98.5\% for the noiseless model. None of the paired differences is
significant at either range. Within 150 steps, the only significant difference
appears at $R=0.468$, and it favors the Pauli twirl (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

What the twirl changes instead is the scale [Fig.~\ref{fig:vr}(c)]. At $p=0.2$,
the Pauli twirl needs 1.24 to 1.57 times the shots of AD, depending on the
range. The Clifford twirl needs 0.92 to 1.16 times the shots of AD, and its
shot factor follows the exact depolarizing value $\lambda_{\rm depol}^{-10}$,
since by Eq.~\eqref{eq:depol_scale} the five insertions shrink the output by
$\lambda_{\rm depol}^5$. On a single qubit, the cost of twirling is therefore
modest. As we show next, it becomes large in the classifier, where it compounds
over qubits and layers.

\subsection{Classifier}
\label{sec:clf}

We now turn to the four-qubit classifier, where the effect of the scale is much
stronger. With the raw readout and 100 training steps, the unital channels at
first seem to lose trainability as the circuit gets deeper
[Fig.~\ref{fig:clf}(a) and Table~\ref{tab:clf} in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}]. Between $L=1$ and $L=4$, the test
accuracy falls from 0.847 to 0.715 for the Pauli twirl and from 0.851 to 0.695
for depolarizing noise, and the training accuracy falls with it, to 0.700 and
0.672. AD stays at 0.84, and the noiseless circuit at 0.86. Taken at face
value, this would suggest that the non-unital term keeps the deep circuit
trainable.

A closer look shows that the drop comes from the readout, not from the
circuit. If we train the same raw readout for 1000 steps, the unital channels
recover to 0.837 and 0.826 at $L=4$. Over these extra steps, the norm of their
readout weights grows from about 19 to 106 and 109, while for the noiseless
circuit it grows only from 8 to 20. The reason is that, after 100 steps, the
unital channels have shrunk the spread to about 5 and 4 percent of the
noiseless spread, and to below 1 percent before training. With a spread this
small, the raw readout cannot grow its weights fast enough. The circuit itself
remains trainable. With the standardized readout, every channel reaches the
accuracy of the noiseless circuit at every depth [Fig.~\ref{fig:clf}(b)], and
the Pauli and depolarizing channels are statistically indistinguishable from it
(Table~\ref{tab:stats}).

We see the same behavior on Fashion-MNIST at $L=4$. With the raw readout, the
unital channels fall 16 and 17 points below the noiseless circuit (0.765), and
AD falls 5 points below it. With the standardized readout, all three channels
come within two points of the noiseless circuit [Fig.~\ref{fig:fashion} in
Appendix~\ref{app:stats}],
while their shot factors are 34 for AD and $9.9\times10^3$ and
$8.2\times10^3$ for the Pauli and depolarizing channels.

The depolarizing channel at $L=1$ gives an exact check of this picture. Each
$z_j$ then crosses all three noise insertions as a weight-one Pauli string,
so the channel multiplies every $z_j$ by the same factor
$\lambda_{\rm depol}^3$, whatever the parameters. Since the standardized
readout removes a uniform scale, the depolarizing and noiseless classifiers
should make identical predictions, and indeed they do, on every test image. AD
is not a pure rescaling, yet with the standardized readout it too stays within
one percentage point of the noiseless accuracy at every depth
(Table~\ref{tab:stats}).

What the standardized readout cannot remove is the difference in spread
[Fig.~\ref{fig:clf}(c)]. At the end of training, the shot factor of the unital
channels grows by a factor of about 14 per layer, from 4.1 at $L=1$ to
$1.1\times10^4$ at $L=4$ for depolarizing noise and to $1.6\times10^4$ for the
Pauli twirl. For AD, it grows by only about 2.4 per layer, from 3.6 to 50. Note
that the shot factor is not a property of the channel alone. It also depends on
what the model is trained for. With the raw readout, the optimizer moves the
parameters toward less contracted expectation values, and after 1000 steps at $L=4$ the
shot factors are 11 for AD, 320 for the Pauli twirl, and 470 for depolarizing
noise.

In summary, the zero-temperature bias acts in our models mainly through the spread of
the expectation values, and the reported loss of expressivity is a loss of
scale. A trained readout absorbs
this loss, but at a cost in shots that grows with depth.

\section{Gauge freedom of the damping direction}
\label{sec:gauge}

So far, we have compared AD with its twirls, which remove the non-unital term
altogether. A simpler comparison only reverses the term, by damping toward
$\ket1$ instead of $\ket0$. In this section, we ask when such a reversal
changes the model at all, and we test the answer in the classifier and in the
eigensolver.

\subsection{When the damping direction is a gauge}
\label{sec:gauge_def}

Amplitude damping toward $\ket{1}$, $\AD^{\rm flip}$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:channels}, differs from $\AD$ only in the sign of $\mathbf t$. As we
show next, whether a circuit can tell the two apart depends on how a Pauli
frame propagates through it.

To see how, we write the reversed circuit as a sequence of elements,
$\Phi^{\rm rev}=E^{\rm rev}_m\circ\dots\circ E^{\rm rev}_1$, where the
elements $E^{\rm rev}_k$ are its gates, encodings, and noise layers. The idea
is to insert the identity, in the form $g_k\circ g_k=\mathrm{id}$, at every cut
$k=0,\dots,m$, that is, before the first element, between neighboring
elements, and after the last one. Each $g_k$ is a Pauli
frame~\cite{knill2005quantum}, either a Pauli conjugation or a Pauli
conjugation combined with complex conjugation in the computational basis,
\begin{equation}
g_k(\rho)=
\begin{cases}
P_k\,\rho\,P_k & \text{(unitary type)},\\
P_k\,\bar\rho\,P_k & \text{(antiunitary type)},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:frame}
\end{equation}
where $P_k$ is a Pauli string and all $g_k$ are of the same type. Grouping
each element with its two neighboring frames, we can rewrite the reversed
circuit as
\begin{align}
\Phi^{\rm rev}&=g_m\circ\tilde E_m\circ\dots\circ\tilde E_1\circ g_0,
\label{eq:framed}\\
\tilde E_k&=g_k\circ E^{\rm rev}_k\circ g_{k-1} .
\label{eq:framed_k}
\end{align}
If every $\tilde E_k$ turns out to be an element of the original model,
possibly with different parameters, the reversed model is the original one in
disguise. Whether this happens can be checked element by element. To state the
conditions precisely, we write $y(x;\theta)$ for the outputs of
the model for an input $x$ and parameters $\theta$, that is, the expectation
values it measures, and $y^{\rm rev}(x;\theta)$ for those of the reversed
model.

\begin{proposition}[Frame gauge]\label{prop:gauge}
Reversing the damping is a reparametrization of the model,
\begin{equation}
y^{\mathrm{rev}}(x;\theta)=\eta\,y(x;\theta')
\label{eq:reparam}
\end{equation}
for all inputs $x$, with a sign $\eta=\pm1$ for each output and $\theta'$ depending only on $\theta$, whenever a frame assignment of one type exists such that
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] at every noise location the frame acts as $X$ or $Y$ on each noisy qubit;
\item[(ii)] every fixed gate maps the incoming frame to a Pauli frame;
\item[(iii)] every data-encoding gate is left invariant;
\item[(iv)] every trainable element is mapped back into its own family;
\item[(v)] the input state is absorbed by the first element on each qubit, and the final frame
maps each measured observable to $\eta$ times itself, with $\eta=-1$ allowed only if the readout
has a learnable sign.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}
\noindent The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:gauge}.

\begin{corollary}[Deterministic frames are gauge]\label{cor:det}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:gauge}, two statements follow:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] a deterministic relabeling of the noise by a Pauli frame or by time
reversal leaves the reachable model class unchanged;
\item[(b)] if, in addition, $\theta\mapsto\theta'$ preserves the distribution of
the initial parameters and the optimizer is equivariant under it, the trained
models under $\AD$ and $\AD^{\rm flip}$ are identically distributed.
\end{itemize}
\end{corollary}

The idea of propagating Pauli operators through a parametrized circuit is not
new. Fontana \textit{et al.}~\cite{fontana2022nontrivial} used it, as the
$\sigma$-pulse method, to find parameter symmetries of a single model. They
showed that these symmetries survive unital Pauli noise but can be broken by
non-unital noise such as amplitude damping, because a frame that crosses the
channel as $X$ or $Y$ maps $\AD$ onto $\AD^{\rm flip}$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:gauge} turns this observation around. Instead of relating
two parameter settings of one noise model, it uses the same propagation to
relate two noise models. It also adds the antiunitary frames and states the
conditions on the encoding, the input, and the readout under which the map is
a reparametrization.

The most useful antiunitary frame is spin time reversal,
\begin{equation}
\Theta=\bigotimes_q(-iY_q)\,K ,
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
with $K$ the complex conjugation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:frame}. Time reversal flips
the sign of every Pauli matrix and of the imaginary unit. As a result, it
leaves every single-qubit rotation
$R_{\mathbf n}(a)=e^{-ia\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_q/2}$, with
$\boldsymbol\sigma_q=(X_q,Y_q,Z_q)$ the Pauli matrices of qubit $q$, unchanged.
The damping, on the other hand, is reversed, because the Kraus operators of
$\AD$ are real:
\begin{equation}
\Theta R_{\mathbf n}(a)\,\Theta^{-1}=R_{\mathbf n}(a),\qquad
\Theta\,\AD\,\Theta^{-1}=\AD^{\rm flip},
\label{eq:theta_inv}
\end{equation}
for any axis $\mathbf n$. This has a strong consequence. Condition (iii) holds
for every single-qubit encoding, whatever its axis, so only the multi-qubit
fixed gates, the placement of the noise relative to them, and the two
boundaries of the circuit can break the gauge.

The single-qubit fits of Sec.~\ref{sec:vr} show what happens when one of the conditions fails. These fits use general
single-qubit blocks, an $R_X$ encoding, noise after every gate, and the output
$f=\langle Z\rangle$ directly. They satisfy every condition except (v). The
frame left after the last channel flips the sign of $\langle Z\rangle$, and
nothing in the model can absorb it. Reversing the damping therefore flips the
sign of the output, up to the parameter map $\theta\mapsto\theta'$. The two
model classes are mirror images of each other, and they differ as sets,
because the last channel confines the output under AD and under reversed AD
to
\begin{equation}
f(x;\theta)\in[2p-1,\,1],\qquad f^{\mathrm{rev}}(x;\theta)\in[-1,\,1-2p] ,
\label{eq:vr_ranges}
\end{equation}
and both upper bounds are attained. The difference is nevertheless easy to
remove: a target ensemble that is symmetric under $f\to-f$, or a trainable
output scale, makes the two channels statistically indistinguishable.

\begin{corollary}[The Pauli twirl is not frame-equivalent to AD]\label{cor:rand}
Pauli twirling replaces $\AD$ by $\frac14\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}P\,\AD\,P$, which by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:twirl_mix} is a convex mixture of frame-equivalent channels. It is not itself frame-equivalent to $\AD$: every (anti)unitary
Pauli frame preserves $\lVert\mathbf t\rVert$, which is $p$ for $\AD$ and $0$ for the twirl.
\end{corollary}

\noindent The frame gauge therefore cannot remove the difference between AD and
its twirl. That difference is set by the non-unital term, of length
$\lVert\mathbf t\rVert=p$, and we quantify its effect in Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}.

\subsection{Classifier}
\label{sec:gauge_clf}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_vqe}
\caption{Relative energy error $(E-E_0)/\lvert E_0\rvert$ of the three-qubit
eigensolver against the noise strength, where $E=\Tr[H\rho(\theta)]$ is the
energy of the optimized state, for AD, reversed AD (hollow markers), and the Pauli twirl
(logarithmic vertical axis). Points are the median of 50 initializations and bars the
interquartile range. (a) The channel acts after the complete $R_{ZZ}$; AD and reversed
AD coincide. (b) The channel acts after each CNOT inside $R_{ZZ}$, as in
Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased}. At $\gamma=0.01$, 20 (a) and 26 (b) of the 50
AD runs end in a higher local minimum, which widens the bars and in (b) raises
the median above its value at $\gamma=0.05$; from small initial angles, AD
reaches 0.016 in (b).}
\label{fig:vqe}
\end{figure*}

We now look at the classifier of Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit} in more detail.
Besides the damping itself, time reversal changes three parts of it: the input
state, the readout, and the ring of CNOTs. Each CNOT of the ring is mapped to
\begin{equation}
\Theta\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,\Theta^{-1}=-\mathrm{CNOT}\,(Z\otimes X),
\label{eq:theta_cnot}
\end{equation}
that is, to itself up to a local Pauli residue on its input side, with $Z$ on
the control and $X$ on the target. Because the noise acts only after the
complete ring, these residues can be moved in front of the ring, where the
preceding trainable blocks absorb them. The first block also absorbs
$\Theta\ket0$, and the readout absorbs the sign of the expectation values.
All five conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:gauge} therefore hold for $R_X$,
$R_Y$, and $R_Z$ encodings and for a generic encoding axis alike, and reversing
the damping is an exact reparametrization, as we confirm to machine precision
(Table~\ref{tab:gauge}). Interestingly, a unitary Pauli frame is enough only when it commutes with
the encoding generator and still flips the damping, which singles out $X$ for
$R_X$ and $Y$ for $R_Y$. For $R_Z$ or a generic axis, only the antiunitary frame
works. If the noise acts after each CNOT of the ring
instead, the $X$ residues would have to cross a noise location, and the
exhaustive search of Table~\ref{tab:gauge} finds no frame of either type.

Both conditions of Corollary~\ref{cor:det}(b) also hold. For each frame of
Table~\ref{tab:gauge}, every Euler angle of the trainable layers maps as
$\theta_k\mapsto\pm\theta_k+m_k\pi$ with $m_k\in\{0,1\}$, and the readout maps
as $W\mapsto-W$ and $\mathbf b\mapsto\mathbf b$. These maps leave the
initialization unchanged, because the model depends on each angle only modulo
$2\pi$ and the Gaussian initialization of $W$ is symmetric. The standardized
readout maps $\boldsymbol\mu\mapsto-\boldsymbol\mu$ and
$\boldsymbol\sigma\mapsto\boldsymbol\sigma$, and Adam without weight decay is
equivariant under sign flips and translations of the coordinates. Trained
classifiers under $\AD$ and $\AD^{\rm flip}$ are therefore identically
distributed over initializations, and the same holds with shot noise, whose
distribution is symmetric under $z\mapsto-z$. Comparing the two damping
directions in our classifier thus carries no information about the sign of the
non-unital term.

The training results agree with this. With the raw readout at $L=4$,
AD$^{\rm flip}$ and AD reach nearly the same accuracy, and 96.7\% of their
individual predictions agree. The expected difference in accuracy is exactly
zero. Their individual
predictions differ only because each pair of runs starts from the same
$\theta$ under both channels, rather than from $\theta$ and $\theta'$. This
makes the comparison a useful null test, because we know the answer in
advance. A two-level bootstrap interval, which resamples both the
initializations and the test images, includes zero, as it should, whereas
an interval that resamples only the test images excludes it
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Resampling the test set alone would therefore
understate the uncertainty.

To find out what happens when the gauge is broken, we place the noise after
each CNOT of the ring, where no frame exists, and train both directions with
the standardized readout at $L=4$. Since this variant applies the channel more
often, we compare its two directions only with each other. They still agree
within the uncertainty in accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}) and on 94.6\% of
the test images. Breaking the gauge thus allows a direction effect, but it does
not guarantee a large one. In the classifier, the trainable layers and the
readout may compensate for it. The eigensolver, with its fixed observable,
cannot, as we show next.

\subsection{Eigensolver}
\label{sec:gauge_vqe}

Finally, we turn to the eigensolver, where the direction effect was
reported. Here the relevant symmetry is the global flip $X^{\otimes3}$,
which leaves the Hamiltonian $H$ invariant. When the noise follows the complete
$R_{ZZ}$, the pair flip $X^{\otimes2}$ commutes with the gate. The flip then
passes through every block, all the way back to the initial $R_Y$ rotations,
where it becomes a simple change of parameters,
\begin{equation}
E_{\AD^{\rm flip}}(\theta)=E_{\AD}(\theta'),\qquad \theta'_q=\pi-\theta_q ,
\label{eq:vqe_gauge}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_q$ is the initial $R_Y$ angle on qubit $q$ and all other angles
are unchanged. As a result, the optimized energies under AD and reversed AD
coincide [Fig.~\ref{fig:vqe}(a)].

The situation changes when the
noise acts after each CNOT. No frame can
then flip the damping after both CNOTs of an $R_{ZZ}$. A frame that acts as $X$
or $Y$ on both qubits after the first CNOT acts as $I$ or $Z$ on the target
qubit after the second, so the map fails (Table~\ref{tab:gauge}). The
optimized energies then separate [Fig.~\ref{fig:vqe}(b)], and the gap grows
with $\gamma$, reaching 0.65 in relative error at $\gamma=0.2$.
We checked that this gap is a property of the
energy landscape and not a failure of the optimizer. It persists for small
initial angles, for the best of the 50 initializations, and for a second
optimizer on an independent simulator (Appendix~\ref{app:training}).
This
reproduces the reported direction effect and traces
it to where the noise sits inside the decomposed gate. It is also the case
that matters in practice: on hardware whose native two-qubit gate is the CNOT,
the damping acts at exactly this location.

Two further checks support
this picture. On an open chain, flipping one sublattice maps $J$ to $-J$ and is
absorbed by the final $R_X$ layer, so the sign of the coupling is itself a
gauge. At $h=2$ on the ring, the ground state is paramagnetic and far from
every state that the final layer can prepare from $\ket{000}$. Even there,
reversed AD still trails AD with the damping inside the gate, and AD still
beats its twirl. Neither effect therefore rests
on the ground state. Finally, the eigensolver shows
the limit of rescaling. Unlike a classifier output, the energy cannot be
rescaled freely, so here the twirl costs accuracy unless a mitigation step
restores the scale~\cite{vovrosh2021simple}.

\section{What the non-unital term changes}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_width}
\caption{Spread $\sigma$ of the expectation values before training against the number of
re-uploading layers at $p=0.3$, for (a) four, (b) six, and (c) eight qubits,
with $n$ principal components as inputs. Markers are the root-mean-square
spread over 300 training images and five parameter draws, shared across depths,
for all four channels. Lines are the exact average over parameters for the
noiseless circuit, AD, and the Pauli twirl (Appendix~\ref{app:width}).}
\label{fig:width}
\end{figure*}

Sections~\ref{sec:scale} and \ref{sec:gauge} showed that a trainable scale
removes most of the difference between AD and its twirls, and that the
direction of the damping is often a gauge. What remains, by
Corollary~\ref{cor:rand}, is the size of the non-unital term. In this section, we show what it changes: the spread of the
expectation values before training, the number of shots needed after training, and how
both depend on the damping strength.

\subsection{Floor of the spread}
\label{sec:floor}

We start before training, where the effect is cleanest, and ask why the
spread under AD behaves differently from that under the twirls. Here we
measure the spread as
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2=\mathbb E_\theta\,\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbf a}\,z_j ,
\label{eq:sigma_rms}
\end{equation}
the variance of the $z_j$ over the inputs, averaged over the qubits and over
random parameters, and we write $\sigma_{\AD}$, $\sigma_{\rm Pauli}$, and
$\sigma_{\rm None}$ for its values under AD, the Pauli twirl, and no noise. At
$p=0.3$, the spread separates the channels with both depth and width
(Fig.~\ref{fig:width}). Under the unital channels, the spread shrinks by a nearly constant factor
per layer at every width. Under AD, in contrast, it settles on a floor after
two or three layers. As the circuit gets wider,
the noiseless spread falls, as expected for a scrambling circuit, but the floor
barely moves. At $n=8$ and $L\ge4$, the AD spread is therefore as large as
the noiseless one.

The floor comes from the
non-unital term. To see this, consider a $Z$ component propagated backward
from the readout. Under AD it becomes $c^2Z+t_zI$, with $t_z=p$, whereas the
Pauli twirl keeps only $c^2Z$. The identity part behaves very differently from
the rest. Its expectation value is 1 in every state, so it reaches the readout
no matter what the earlier layers do. The twirl has no such part, and every
component keeps shrinking from layer to layer. At strong damping, the
branching into the identity after the last entangler sets the floor. Averaging
its contribution over the parameters (Appendix~\ref{app:width}), we obtain the
closed form
\begin{multline}
\sigma_\infty^2=\frac{p^2(1-p)^2}{n}\sum_j\Bigl[\frac{(1-p)^2}{2}\,
\mathrm{Var}(\cos a_j)\\
+\frac{1-p}{4}\,\mathrm{Var}(\sin a_j)\Bigr],
\label{eq:floor_var}
\end{multline}
which at $p=0.3$ matches the floor of the AD spread to within 0.5\% for $n\ge4$.

This floor fits well with what is known about noise-induced concentration.
Unital noise concentrates local expectation values exponentially in
depth~\cite{wang2021noiseinduced,schumann2024emergence}, whereas under
non-unital noise their variance over random circuits stays bounded from below
by an amount set by the non-unital term~\cite{mele2026noise}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:floor_var} is the counterpart of that bound for the
variance over inputs, and both scale as $\lVert\mathbf t\rVert^2=p^2$ without
decaying with depth.

The floor also sets how the zero-temperature bias depends on temperature. At
finite temperature, the damping of a qubit with splitting $\hbar\omega$ relaxes
toward the thermal excited-state population
$N=(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}+1)^{-1}$, and at fixed $p$, that is, at fixed
measured $T_1$, Sec.~\ref{sec:channels} gives
\begin{equation}
t_z=p(1-2N)=p\tanh\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_BT} .
\label{eq:tz}
\end{equation}
The floor therefore falls linearly in $1-2N$ as the temperature rises and
vanishes at $N=\frac12$, the Pauli twirl (Appendix~\ref{app:width}). In
summary, the identity part of the non-unital term keeps the AD spread on a
floor at every depth and width, whereas the twirled spreads keep shrinking.

\subsection{Sampling cost}
\label{sec:shots}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_shots}
\caption{Finite shots in the MNIST classifier at $p=0.3$. (a) Test accuracy at
$L=4$ of models trained and tested with $N_s$ shots per image (median and
interquartile range over eight initializations). (b), (c) Operational shot
cost relative to the noiseless circuit (geometric mean over initializations,
with 95\% bootstrap intervals over initializations) for models trained with
exact expectation values (b) and with $N_s=10^3$ shots (c).}
\label{fig:shots}
\end{figure*}

We next ask whether the floor matters for the number of shots that a trained
classifier needs, the operational shot cost of Sec.~\ref{sec:model_clf}. To
answer this, we train under shot noise [Fig.~\ref{fig:shots}(a) and (c)].
Trained and tested with $10^3$ shots per image, AD stays within 1.5 percentage
points of the noiseless accuracy at every depth, while the twirled channels
lose up to 33 points at $L=4$. Part of this loss already arises in training.
Evaluated without shot noise, the twirled models trained with shots remain
clearly less accurate than those trained exactly, whereas AD loses almost
nothing. After training with shots, the operational cost of AD stays close to
its shot factor $\kappa$, whereas for the twirls it still grows by a factor of
3 to 5 per layer [Fig.~\ref{fig:shots}(c)].

The reason is that training under shot noise moves the circuit toward
expectation values whose spread exceeds the shot noise $1/\sqrt{N_s}$. Under
AD, the smallest spread rises to the floor of Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}, above the
shot noise, while under the twirls it stays below it
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

Without shot noise in training, $\kappa$ follows the cost of the unital
channels but misses that of AD [Fig.~\ref{fig:shots}(b)]. At $L=4$, AD then
needs 19 times more shots than its shot factor predicts, because the exactly
trained readout relies on expectation values of very small spread, as a
readout-weighted noise gain shows (Table~\ref{tab:gain} in
Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Even so, the Pauli twirl still needs 18 times the
shots of AD. The floor thus matters for the shot budget. It lets a model
trained under shot noise keep a cost close to $\kappa$, while the twirled
models pay a cost that grows with every layer.

\subsection{Dependence on the damping strength}
\label{sec:pdep}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_sep_main}
\caption{Separation depth, at which the Pauli twirl needs ten times the shots
of AD before training, against the damping strength $p$ for four (filled) and
eight (hollow) qubits. Markers are the exact average over parameters (5000
image pairs at $n=4$, 700 at $n=8$, computed for $p\ge0.01$ at $n=8$). Gray
lines are the estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Lstar} in Appendix~\ref{app:width}.}
\label{fig:sep}
\end{figure}

So far we have worked at strong damping, $p=0.3$. We now turn to weaker
damping, closer to current hardware, and ask at which depth the difference
between AD and its twirls appears. Both sides of the comparison change with
$p$. The twirled spread decays more slowly per layer, and the floor of the AD
spread gains a contribution from earlier layers (Appendix~\ref{app:width}).
To quantify when the two spreads separate, we define the separation depth as
the depth at which the squared ratio of the AD and Pauli-twirl spreads first
reaches 10. There, the twirl needs ten times the shots of AD before training.
At weak damping, the separation depth grows roughly as $p^{-1}\ln(1/p)$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sep}), in close agreement with a simple estimate that we derive
in Appendix~\ref{app:width}.

Trained classifiers follow this picture (Table~\ref{tab:pdep_trained} in
Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Well below the separation depth, AD and its twirls
reach the same accuracy at nearly the same shot cost, and at the separation
depth the twirls need about four times the shots of AD.

This has a clear consequence for real devices. On superconducting hardware,
the damping
is of order $10^{-3}$ per two-qubit gate, for gate times of 30 to 400~ns and
$T_1$ of order $100~\mu$s~\cite{krantz2019quantum}. In our model, one of the
two insertions per layer follows a ring of $n$ CNOTs, so $p$ per insertion
corresponds to several gate durations. At $p=10^{-3}$, the separation needs
about 1500 layers at $n=4$ (Table~\ref{tab:pdep} in Appendix~\ref{app:width}). At realistic damping, the
difference between AD and its twirls therefore appears only in very deep
circuits.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have compared amplitude damping with its twirls, in particular with the
Pauli twirl, which is damping at infinite temperature. In our models, the
zero-temperature bias acts mainly through the spread of the expectation values.
Under AD, the spread stops at a floor fixed at strong damping by the last layer,
and at fixed $T_1$ this floor falls linearly in $1-2N$ as the temperature
rises. Under the twirls, it keeps shrinking by a constant factor per layer at
every width up to eight qubits. Training an output scale removes most of the
resulting differences in fit and accuracy in exact simulation. What it removes reappears as a sampling cost: at a fixed shot budget, the
classifier under AD stays close to its noiseless accuracy, while the twirled
classifiers fall far behind. At weak damping, the two channels separate only
after many layers, so the difference matters at strong damping or in very deep
circuits.

The direction of the damping is a gauge, that is, reversing it changes only
the parameters, whenever the damping follows complete entangling layers and
trainable elements at both ends of the circuit absorb the flip. With a symmetric initialization and an
equivariant optimizer, the trained models under the two directions are then
identically distributed, as in our classifier. The direction can matter only
where no frame can be absorbed, as in the eigensolver with the damping inside
the decomposed two-qubit gate.

These results suggest three practices for comparing noise structures in
variational models. First, report the output scale, or train one, together
with the shot budget it implies. Second, before attributing an effect to the
direction of the noise, check whether the model class is invariant under the
relevant frames. Third, state where the noise acts relative to the
decomposition of the gates.

Several questions remain open. Our simulations are exact and small: one qubit
for the fits, four for the classifier, and three for the eigensolver. We
established the width dependence before training, up to eight qubits, but
training beyond four qubits was out of reach of exact simulation, and our shot
model in training neglects the correlations between qubits. It also remains
to be seen whether trained models follow the temperature dependence of the
floor, and whether the results hold on hardware, where damping comes with
dephasing.

\paragraph{Setting.}
A model on $n$ qubits prepares $\rho_0=\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes n}$, applies elements
$E_1,\dots,E_m$, and reads out
\begin{equation}
y_i=\Tr\bigl[O_i\,\Phi(\rho_0)\bigr],\qquad \Phi=E_m\circ\dots\circ E_1 ,
\label{eq:app_model}
\end{equation}
possibly followed by a learned affine map. Each $E_k$ is a fixed unitary $\mathcal U(U)$, a data encoding
$\mathcal U\bigl(S(x)\bigr)$ with $S(x)=\bigotimes_q R_{\mathbf n_q}(x_q)$, a trainable unitary from a
family $\mathcal V$ (general single-qubit blocks, or Pauli rotations $e^{-i\theta Q/2}$), or a
layer of the single-qubit channel $\AD$ on a set $S_k$ of qubits. The reversed model
replaces every noise layer by $\AD^{\rm flip}$.

\paragraph{Frames.}
Let $\mathcal P(\rho)=P\rho P$ for a Pauli string $P$, and $\mathcal K(\rho)=\bar\rho$. On Hermitian
operators $\mathcal K$ is real-linear, trace preserving and positive, $\mathcal K^2=\mathrm{id}$,
and for any channel $\mathcal E$ with Kraus operators $\{K_j\}$ one has
$\mathcal K\circ\mathcal E\circ\mathcal K=\bar{\mathcal E}$, the channel with Kraus operators
$\{\bar K_j\}$. The frames of Eq.~\eqref{eq:frame} are $g=\mathcal P$ for the unitary type and
$g=\mathcal P\circ\mathcal K$ for the antiunitary type. In both cases $g\circ g=\mathrm{id}$, and
\begin{equation}
g\circ\mathcal E\circ g'\colon\ \{K_j\}\mapsto\bigl\{P\,K_j^{(t)}\,P'\bigr\},
\label{eq:app_frame}
\end{equation}
with $K^{(t)}=K$ for the unitary type and $K^{(t)}=\bar K$ for the antiunitary type.

\paragraph{Telescoping.}
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:framed} and \eqref{eq:framed_k} hold for any frames
$g_0,\dots,g_m$ of one type, since every inserted $g_k\circ g_k$ is the identity.

\paragraph{The conditions.}
(i) Consider a noise layer on $S_k$ with $g_k=g_{k-1}$ acting as $X$ or $Y$ on each
$q\in S_k$. The Kraus operators of $\AD$ are real, and
$X\AD X=Y\AD Y=\AD^{\rm flip}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:channels}), so for both types
\begin{equation}
g_k\circ\AD^{\rm flip}\circ g_{k-1}=\AD .
\label{eq:app_i}
\end{equation}
Off $S_k$ the frame passes unchanged.

(ii) For a fixed unitary $U$, $g_k\circ\mathcal U(U)\circ g_{k-1}=\mathcal U\bigl(P_kU^{(t)}P_{k-1}\bigr)$, which
equals $\mathcal U(U)$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
P_k\propto U\,P_{k-1}\,U^{(t)\dagger} .
\label{eq:app_ii}
\end{equation}
The frame thus propagates by conjugation and must remain a Pauli string. For Clifford $U$ this
holds for every $P_{k-1}$ of the unitary type; for real Clifford $U$ (CNOT) also of the
antiunitary type.

(iii) For an encoding, $g_k=g_{k-1}$ and $P_q R^{(t)}_{\mathbf n_q}(x)P_q=R_{\mathbf n_q}(x)$ for
all $x$. For the unitary type this requires $[P_q,\mathbf n_q\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_q]=0$. For
the antiunitary type, $\overline{R_{\mathbf n_q}(x)}=\exp[+ix\,\overline{\mathbf n_q\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_q}/2]$,
so the condition is
\begin{equation}
P_q\,\overline{\mathbf n_q\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_q}\,P_q=-\mathbf n_q\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_q ,
\label{eq:app_iii}
\end{equation}
which holds for $P_q=Y$ and every $\mathbf n_q$, since $\bar X=X$, $\bar Y=-Y$, $\bar Z=Z$,
$YXY=-X$, $YZY=-Z$, and $YYY=Y$.

(iv) A general single-qubit block $V$ is mapped to $P_{k,q}V^{(t)}P_{k-1,q}\in SU(2)$ for any
pair of frames: such blocks absorb, and may change, the frame on their qubits. A Pauli
rotation is mapped to
\begin{equation}
P\,e^{\mp i\theta Q^{(t)}/2}\,P=e^{-i\theta' Q/2},\qquad \theta'=\pm\theta ,
\label{eq:app_iv}
\end{equation}
if and only if $g_k=g_{k-1}$ and $PQ^{(t)}P=\pm Q$; it can carry a frame but not change it.

(v) By Eq.~\eqref{eq:framed} the input is $g_0(\rho_0)=P_0\rho_0P_0$ (as $\bar\rho_0=\rho_0$).
If the first element on qubit $q$ is a general block $V$, the pair $V^{(t)}P_{0,q}$ is itself
a general block acting on $\ket 0$; if it is $R_X$ or $R_Y$, $X\ket0$ and $Y\ket0$ equal
$R_{X,Y}(\pi)\ket0$ up to phase and are absorbed by a shift of the angle; otherwise $P_{0,q}$
must fix $\ket0$, i.e. $P_{0,q}\in\{I,Z\}$. At the output, using
$\Tr[O\bar\tau]=\Tr[\bar O\tau]$ for Hermitian arguments,
\begin{equation}
\Tr\bigl[O\,g_m(\tau)\bigr]=\Tr\bigl[P_mO^{(t)}P_m\,\tau\bigr],
\label{eq:app_v}
\end{equation}
for any state $\tau$, which is $\eta\,\Tr[O\tau]$ if and only if $P_mO^{(t)}P_m=\eta O$.

\paragraph{Completing the proof.}
If (i)--(v) hold, every $\tilde E_k$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:framed} is an element of the original
model with reparametrized trainable parameters, the input frame is absorbed by the first
elements and the output frame contributes the sign $\eta$; hence
\begin{equation}
y^{\mathrm{rev}}_i(x;\theta)=\eta_i\,y_i(x;\theta')
\end{equation}
for all $x$, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:reparam}. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Corollaries.}
Statement (a) of Corollary~\ref{cor:det} follows because $\theta\mapsto\theta'$ is a
bijection of the parameter space. For (b), write the map as
$\theta'=D_\theta\theta+\pi\mathbf m$, with $D_\theta$ a diagonal sign matrix and
$\mathbf m$ an integer vector, as for every frame of Table~\ref{tab:gauge},
together with $W\mapsto W\,\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\eta)$ for the readout, where
$\boldsymbol\eta$ collects the signs $\eta_i$. The training losses $\mathcal C$
under $\AD$ and $\mathcal C^{\rm flip}$ under $\AD^{\rm flip}$ then satisfy
$\mathcal C^{\rm flip}(D_\theta\theta+\pi\mathbf m,\,W\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\eta),\,\mathbf b)=\mathcal C(\theta,W,\mathbf b)$,
so their gradients transform with $D_\theta$ and $\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\eta)$.
An optimizer whose update commutes with these maps (gradient descent and Adam
without weight decay exactly, SPSA in distribution) carries the trajectory from
$(\theta_0,W_0,\mathbf b_0)$ under $\AD$ onto the trajectory from
$(D_\theta\theta_0+\pi\mathbf m,\,W_0\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\eta),\,\mathbf b_0)$
under $\AD^{\rm flip}$. If the map preserves the distribution of
$(\theta_0,W_0,\mathbf b_0)$, the trained models are identically distributed.

For Corollary~\ref{cor:rand}, write a qubit channel as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch}.
Unitary Pauli conjugation and complex conjugation act on $(T,\mathbf t)$ as
\begin{align}
\mathcal P\colon&\ (T,\mathbf t)\mapsto(DTD,\,D\mathbf t),
\label{eq:app_D}\\
\mathcal K\colon&\ (T,\mathbf t)\mapsto(FTF,\,F\mathbf t),
\label{eq:app_F}
\end{align}
with $D$ a diagonal sign matrix and $F=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1)$; both preserve
$\lVert\mathbf t\rVert$. The Pauli twirl has $\mathbf t=0$, while
$\lVert\mathbf t\rVert=p$ for amplitude damping.

\paragraph{Time reversal and CNOT.}
A $Z$ residue of Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta_cnot}
passes through amplitude damping ($Z\AD Z=\AD$); an $X$ residue does not. The residue must
therefore reach a general trainable block without crossing a noise location, which is the
case in our classifier (the trainable layer immediately precedes the ring) and fails when
noise is inserted after each CNOT (Table~\ref{tab:gauge}).

\paragraph{Scope.}
The proposition is a sufficient condition. The rows marked ``none'' in
Table~\ref{tab:gauge} establish that no frame of either type exists; they do not by
themselves prove that the classes differ. For the single-qubit fits, the output
ranges of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vr_ranges} prove it. For the eigensolver at $\gamma=0.3$, with L-BFGS-B
from 12 random initializations and the channel applied once after each
$R_{ZZ}$ or after each CNOT, the lowest energies are equal where the gauge
holds ($-3.1770$ for both) and differ by 1.98 where it does not ($-3.087$
against $-1.109$), which reproduces the direction of the reported effect. For the
classifier with noise inside the
ring, Sec.~\ref{sec:gauge_clf} measures the difference directly.

factor only looks at the average spread of the $z_j$, whereas the readout

\begin{equation}

\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}

runs over the test images. If the shot noise of different qubits is
uncorrelated, $G$ is $N_s$ times the total shot-noise variance of the class
scores. Table~\ref{tab:gain} compares the exact-training costs with $G$ and
with the shot factor. For $L\ge2$, $G$ reproduces the measured cost ratios to
within a factor of 1.6.

\section{Spread before training}
\label{app:width}

In this appendix, we derive the floor of the AD spread and its dependence on
the damping strength, and we define the separation depth used in
Sec.~\ref{sec:pdep}.

\paragraph{Exact average.}
The lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:width} are exact averages, over parameters drawn
uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$, of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_rms}, where the variance
runs over the first 300 training images. We compute them in
the Heisenberg picture from the second moments of the Pauli-string
coefficients of $Z_j$, with the parameters shared between two images. Every
uniformly random $R_ZR_YR_Z$ block removes the cross moments between different
strings because, averaged over its angles, the entries $R_{bc}$ of its
adjoint action have vanishing first moments and different columns are
uncorrelated, $\mathbb E_\theta[R_{ab}R_{a'c}]=0$ for $b\neq c$. Between two
blocks the circuit acts site by site (channel, $R_X(a_j)$, channel), so the
second moments of two images propagate through the elementwise product of the
two composite site maps, followed by the entangling ring, which permutes the
strings up to a sign. The second moments therefore obey a closed recursion, which is exact up
to the sampling of image pairs: all pairs for $n\le5$, and 5000 random pairs
for $n\ge6$. Against brute-force averages of the density-matrix simulation,
the recursion agrees within one to two standard errors in all 18 cells at
$n=2$ and 3 and $L=1$ to 3. An independent density-matrix simulation at $n=4$, with 200 parameter draws, agrees within two standard errors in all 12 cells for the noiseless circuit, AD, and the Pauli twirl at $L=1$ to 4, and in all four cells for AD and the Pauli twirl at $p=0.1$ and $L=4$ and 8.

The measured points in
Fig.~\ref{fig:width} use five parameter draws, the same parameter vectors at
every depth (those of depth $L$ are the leading entries of those of depth 6),
so their deviations are correlated across $L$. The distribution over the
parameters is heavy-tailed, and five draws tend to fall below the average, as
for the Pauli twirl at $n=8$ and $L\ge4$. An independent Pauli-path simulation
reproduces these five draws to $2\times10^{-12}$ and, over 120 further draws
at $L=4$, agrees with the exact average within one standard error. In this
exact average, the ratio of the AD and Pauli-twirl spreads grows by a factor
of 3.3 per layer at $n=4$ and 4.5 for $n\ge6$, and its square, the ratio of
their shot factors, by 11 and 20. At $n=4$, the squared ratio is 2.1 at $L=1$
and $1.3\times10^3$ at $L=4$.

\paragraph{Floor at strong damping.}
Under AD the adjoint channel maps $Z$ to $c^2Z+pI$. The next random block
averages the cross moments between the $I$ and $Z$ branches to zero. The AD
spread therefore splits into the twirled part and a branching part,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\AD}^2=\sigma_{\rm Pauli}^2+\sigma_{\rm br}^2 ,
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\rm br}$ collects the paths through at least one branching into
the identity. The damping after the last entangler branches the $Z$ component
of each $z_j$ into the identity and adds
\begin{equation}
\delta z_j=p(1-p)\bigl[(1-p)\,v_z\cos a_j-c\,v_y\sin a_j\bigr]
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
to $z_j$, where $(v_x,v_y,v_z)$ is the direction into which the final
trainable block rotates $Z_j$. Averaged over the final block, with
$\mathbb E\,v_z^2=\frac12$, $\mathbb E\,v_y^2=\frac14$, and
$\mathbb E\,v_zv_y=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} yields Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor_var}.
The agreement within 0.5\% quoted in Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} uses all image
pairs. For uniformly distributed angles, $\sigma_\infty=0.096$. At strong
damping, branchings in earlier layers are attenuated by the rest of the
circuit, so the last layer alone sets the floor.

\paragraph{Weak damping.}
At weak damping, the twirled spread decays per layer as $e^{-np}$. This is
because a scrambled Pauli string has about $3n/4$ non-identity sites, and each
of the two insertions per layer shrinks such a site by a factor of about
$1-2p/3$ on average. This decay rate agrees with $np$ within 1\% for
$p\le0.05$ at $n=4$ and 8. The floor of the AD spread at large depth,
$\sigma_{\rm br,\infty}$, changes as well (Table~\ref{tab:pdep} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:sep_app}). Its square has two parts,
$\sigma_{\rm br,\infty}^2\approx\sigma_\infty^2+b_{\rm br}\,p$. The first is
proportional to $p^2$ [Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor_var}]. The second comes from
branchings in the roughly $1/(np)$ earlier layers that the damping does not yet
suppress. It acts on the input dependence of those layers, which concentrates
with width. For $p\le0.03$, $b_{\rm br}$ is
$(1.8$--$2.7)\times10^{-3}$ at $n=4$ and $(1.0$--$2.4)\times10^{-4}$ at
$n=8$, about an order of magnitude smaller.

\paragraph{Separation depth.}
We define the separation depth $L^*$ as the depth at which
$(\sigma_{\AD}/\sigma_{\rm Pauli})^2$ first reaches 10. There, the twirl needs
ten times the shots of AD before training. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:split}, this
happens when $\sigma_{\rm br}=3\,\sigma_{\rm Pauli}$. With
$\sigma_{\rm br}\approx\sigma_{\rm br,\infty}$ and
$\sigma_{\rm Pauli}\approx\sigma_{\rm None}\,e^{-npL}$, we obtain the estimate
\begin{equation}
L^*\approx\frac{1}{np}\,\ln\frac{3\,\sigma_{\rm None}}{\sigma_{\rm br,\infty}},
\label{eq:Lstar}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\rm None}$ is taken at large depth (0.24 at $n=4$ and 0.062 at
$n=8$). Equation~\eqref{eq:Lstar} agrees with the exact separation depth
within 1\% for $p\le0.01$ and within one layer for larger $p$ at $n=4$, and
within two layers for $p\le0.03$ at $n=8$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sep}). Since
$\sigma_{\rm br,\infty}$ falls roughly as a power of $p$ and enters only
through the logarithm, $L^*$ grows roughly as $p^{-1}\ln(1/p)$.

\paragraph{Finite temperature.}
At finite temperature, the branching part of the
spread at $n=4$ follows $\lvert1-2N\rvert$ within 0.4\% for every $N$ and
$L\le8$ at $p=0.3$, and within 0.2\% at $p=0.1$. It is thus symmetric under
$N\leftrightarrow1-N$, as the frame gauge of Sec.~\ref{sec:gauge} requires.

\section{Training details and numerical checks}
\label{app:training}

\paragraph{Single-qubit fits.}
Where Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased} leaves a choice, we chose as follows. The
250 inputs lie on a uniform grid over $[-2\pi,2\pi]$, and the target
coefficients are drawn uniformly from $[-1,1]$. Each target is shifted to zero
mid-range and rescaled to range $R$ on this grid. A step is one Adam update on a
batch of 25 inputs, with a seeded shuffle renewed every ten steps and shared by
all channels, and with their settings for $L=2$
(learning rate 0.20, $\beta_1=0.45$, $\beta_2=0.95$). Initial parameters are
drawn uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$, the same for every channel. The largest
converging range is found by bisection on $[0,2]$ with ten halvings. Their free
target offset is equivalent to a trainable
$b$ in $f=\langle Z\rangle+b$. The rescaled model starts from $s=1$ and $b=0$
and is trained for up to 1000 steps with the same Adam settings.

\paragraph{Classifier training.}
To prepare the inputs, we standardize the pixel values, compute four principal
components, and map each component linearly to an angle, sending its minimum
to 0 and its maximum to $2\pi$. The standardization, the principal components,
and the extrema are fitted on 1000 training images and applied unchanged to all
3037 test images of the three digits. Training minimizes the softmax cross-entropy with Adam~\cite{kingma2014adam} at
learning rate $0.05$ for 100 full-batch steps unless stated otherwise, without
weight decay. The circuit angles start uniformly in $[0,2\pi)$, the readout
weights from a zero-mean normal distribution with standard deviation 0.5, and
the bias at zero. The density matrix
is simulated exactly in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}. No regularizing
constant is added to $\boldsymbol\sigma$ in the standardized readout. In one
run, the spread of one $z_j$ fell to $5\times10^{-5}$, and the training diverged
(Table~\ref{tab:clf}). The Fashion-MNIST classes are T-shirt, pullover, and
coat, with 3000 test images. For reference, logistic regression, a
$k$-nearest-neighbor classifier ($k=15$), and a support-vector machine with a
radial kernel, trained on the same angles, reach 0.856, 0.879, and 0.886 on
MNIST and 0.733, 0.758, and 0.748 on Fashion-MNIST. With the explicit parameter maps of
Sec.~\ref{sec:gauge_clf}, the identity $y^{\rm rev}(x;\theta)=\eta\,y(x;\theta')$
holds to $9.4\times10^{-16}$ for $n=2$ to 4 and $L=1$ to 4.

\paragraph{Shot model.}
For the finite-shot runs, each image is measured $N_s$ times in the
computational basis, and the estimate $\hat z_j$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:shotvar} is the
mean of the outcomes $\pm1$ of qubit $j$. At test time we sample the outcomes from the exact distribution of
the measured state, five times per model, and in every draw we re-estimate
$\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\sigma$ from $N_s$ shots of each training
image, as one would on hardware. In training we replace each $z_j$ by
\begin{equation}
\hat z_j=z_j+\sqrt{\frac{1-z_j^2}{N_s}}\;\varepsilon_j ,
\label{eq:shot_train}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_j$ is a standard normal random number, redrawn at every step
and for every image, and compute $\boldsymbol\mu$ and
$\boldsymbol\sigma$ from the noisy $\hat z_j$; gradients flow through $z_j$. This
Gaussian model has the exact shot variance of each $z_j$ but neglects the
correlations between qubits and the shot noise of parameter-shift gradients. We train with
$N_s=10^3$ at $L=1$ to 4, and with $N_s=10^2$ and $10^4$ at $L=4$. The
operational shot cost is interpolated in $\log N_s$ on a grid from 10 to
$10^8$. It is undefined for three exactly trained models at $L=1$, which we
omit: the diverged Pauli run, and one noiseless and one depolarizing model
whose accuracy stays more than 0.01 below its exact value up to $10^8$ shots.

\paragraph{Eigensolver.}
The eigensolver is trained with Adam at learning rate 0.05 for 500 steps, from
parameters drawn uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$. The reflection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vqe_gauge} preserves
the uniform initialization of the $R_Y$ angles on $[0,\pi]$ used in
Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased}, but their optimizer,
COBYLA~\cite{powell1994direct}, builds its first simplex along the positive
coordinate axes and is not equivariant under it, so for that optimizer only the
reachable energies are guaranteed to coincide. For the inside placement, our
implementation reproduces the energies of the open
Qiskit~\cite{javadiabhari2024quantum} code~\cite{vanrossum2026code} of
Ref.~\cite{vanrossum2026biased} to
$4\times10^{-14}$ for the noiseless circuit, AD, reversed AD, and both twirls,
at $\gamma\in\{0.01,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$.
With the damping after each complete $R_{ZZ}$, the two
sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vqe_gauge} agree to $1.4\times10^{-15}$ over 50 random
parameter sets at every $\gamma$, and trajectories started from mapped initial
parameters end at energies that agree to $6\times10^{-15}$. With the damping
after each CNOT, the energies of the two channels at mapped parameters differ
by 0.10 to 0.66 between $\gamma=0.01$ and 0.2, and the median relative error of
reversed AD exceeds that of AD by 0.06, 0.38, 0.56, and 0.65 at $\gamma=0.01$,
0.05, 0.1, and 0.2. Small initial angles, with standard deviation 0.1 about zero,
give the same gaps within 0.01 at $\gamma\ge0.05$. The best of the 50
initializations for reversed AD lies within 0.01 of its median and above the
median for AD at every $\gamma$. L-BFGS-B~\cite{byrd1995limited} from six
further random initializations, run on an independently written simulator,
reaches the same minima at $\gamma=0.2$ (relative errors 0.0370 for AD and
0.6881 for reversed AD).

\paragraph{Coupling sign and field strength.}
On the open chain, with $B$ the middle qubit, the energies of the two couplings
agree at the mapped parameters to $2\times10^{-15}$ for every channel and
placement, and with $h=0.5$ and $\gamma=0.1$ reversed AD reaches a median
relative error of 0.367 against 0.063 for AD for both $J=\pm1$. At $h=2$ on the
ring and $\gamma=0.1$, every state that the final $R_X$ layer can prepare from
$\ket{000}$ has a relative error of at least 0.54. There, AD reaches 0.29 and
0.23 with the damping inside and after the gate, against 0.59 inside the gate
for reversed AD and 0.66 and 0.59 for the twirl. On the ring at $h=0.5$ and $\gamma=0.1$, the Pauli
twirl gives the largest error in both placements, 0.74 inside and 0.70 after.
